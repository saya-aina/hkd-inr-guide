The stuff that trips people up

Bona fide purposes and RBI purpose codes

Every inbound remittance to India is tagged with an RBI purpose code by the receiving bank. For personal transfers to an NRE/NRO account the bank handles this quietly; for larger or less typical amounts, they may ask you (or the sender) to confirm the purpose.

The codes that cover almost all HK → India personal use:

CodePurposeTypical use
P1301Family maintenance & savingsSending home to parents, spouse, children, or your own NRE savings
P1306Personal gifts & donationsBirthday, wedding, festival gifts to extended family
P1308InheritanceTransfer of inherited funds
P0802Software/professional servicesFreelance or consulting income from HK clients
P1007Travel-relatedReimbursement of travel costs
Rule of thumb: if you're an NRI sending your own HK salary to your own NRE account, you don't need to do anything — the sending provider and receiving bank handle the code. The purpose only becomes a conversation if the amount or pattern looks unusual.

NRE vs NRO vs Resident Savings — which account should the money land in?

This is the single most important decision for an NRI, and most providers let you choose at the moment you add the beneficiary. Pick the wrong one and you create tax problems.

AccountWho uses itRepatriable?Tax on interest
NRE (Non-Resident External)NRIs saving foreign-earned incomeYes, fullyTax-free in India
NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary)NRIs with India-sourced income (rent, dividends)Up to USD 1M/yearTaxable (TDS applies)
Resident SavingsResident IndiansSubject to LRS rulesTaxable

The trap: if you've moved to Hong Kong but never converted your old resident savings account to NRO, you're technically in FEMA violation every time money lands there. Convert first, remit second.

The other trap: some low-cost providers only route to resident savings accounts. For an NRI that's wrong on day one — the money arrives but it's parked in the wrong vehicle for tax and repatriation. The filter above hides providers that can't do NRE.

FIRC and eFIRC — when you actually need the paperwork

A Foreign Inward Remittance Certificate (now mostly issued electronically as eFIRC) is the receiving bank's proof that money came from outside India. It's not needed for every transfer, but when you need it, you need it.

You'll want eFIRC for:

  • Home loan down payment proof — Indian lenders will ask for FIRC to confirm the source of your contribution.
  • Tax filing — if you're declaring foreign-source income or claiming DTAA benefits.
  • Property purchase — the registrar may ask for proof of foreign remittance.
  • Freelancer/exporter GST compliance — eFIRC is a key document for export of services.
  • Future repatriation from NRE/NRO — proves the funds were originally remitted from abroad.

Not every provider issues eFIRC smoothly. Bank-to-bank wires always do (your receiving bank issues it). Fintechs vary — Wise and Instarem will issue on request, usually within 2–5 business days. Some aggregators route through partner banks and the eFIRC comes from the partner, which can take longer and requires chasing. The filter above hides providers that don't issue it reliably.

Cutoff times, weekends, and holiday lag

"Instant" is a half-truth on this corridor. The HKD leg, the FX conversion, and the INR leg each have their own cutoffs — and the slowest one determines when your money actually arrives.

Hong Kong side (funding)

  • FPS (Faster Payment System): 24/7, HKD funds reach the provider in seconds. This is almost always the fastest funding method.
  • Local HKD transfer / CHATS: business hours only; after 5pm HKT or weekends, it waits until the next business day.
  • Debit/credit card: instant but adds 0.5–2% in card processing cost on top of the FX margin.

FX conversion

  • Interbank FX markets close Friday 5pm New York (Saturday 5am HKT) and reopen Sunday 5pm New York (Monday 6am HKT).
  • Providers that queue FX over the weekend will convert at Monday morning rates — if HKD weakens over the weekend, you lose. Some lock the rate at the moment you send; check this.

India side (delivery)

  • IMPS: 24/7, instant to the recipient's bank account. The gold standard for small-to-mid transfers.
  • NEFT: 24/7 since 2019, settles in half-hourly batches. Fine for larger amounts.
  • RTGS: 24/7 since 2020, instant settlement, used for high-value (above INR 2 lakh).
  • Public holidays: all three systems operate, but recipient banks may delay credit on major holidays (Diwali, Holi, Republic Day). Private banks are usually faster than PSU banks here.
Practical timing: If you send via Wise or Instarem using FPS on a weekday morning HKT, money lands in a private-bank NRE account within 2–4 hours. Same transfer on Saturday evening? Expect Monday afternoon.

iAM Smart — HK's digital ID and why it matters for onboarding

iAM Smart is the Hong Kong government's digital identity service, launched in late 2020. If you've registered (via the app plus a one-time registration at a self-service kiosk or participating bank), it functions as a digitally-verified HKID.

The upshot for remittance onboarding: a provider integrated with iAM Smart can KYC you in under five minutes with no HKID photo upload, no proof-of-address wait, no manual review queue. For HK residents in serviced apartments, shared housing, or company-provided accommodation — where "proof of address" is a recurring headache — this is a material time saver.

Integration status (verify directly before relying on this):

  • Local HK banks (HSBC HK, Hang Seng, BOCHK, DBS HK, Standard Chartered HK): most support iAM Smart for account opening, which indirectly means their remittance product does too.
  • International fintechs (Wise, Instarem, Remitly, OrbitRemit): typically do not integrate directly; they run their own KYC flow with HKID upload. Still fast (10–20 minutes) but requires document photos.
  • Panda Remit, WorldRemit, XE: own KYC, no iAM Smart.

If instant onboarding matters more to you than an extra 0.2% on the FX margin, the HK-bank remittance route via iAM Smart is worth the small premium.

High-value transfers — above HKD 120,000

HKD 120,000 is a soft threshold on this corridor. It's not a legal limit (there's no HKMA cap on outbound personal remittance), but it's the amount above which most providers trigger enhanced due diligence (EDD): a one-time additional check that can delay a first-time high-value transfer by hours or days.

What EDD looks like:

  • Source-of-funds documentation: payslip, employment letter, or sale deed for the amount.
  • Purpose confirmation: a dropdown or free-text explanation of why you're sending this amount.
  • Beneficiary verification: extra checks on the receiving account, sometimes a confirmation call.

Providers handle this very differently:

  • HK banks (HSBC, DBS, BOCHK): handle high-value smoothly because you're already KYC'd for the full relationship. Often the right choice for one-off large transfers.
  • Wise, Instarem: automated EDD, usually resolved same day on second attempt if docs are clean.
  • Smaller fintechs: may put you in a manual review queue for 1–3 days. Fine if you planned ahead, painful if you didn't.

On the India side, there is no cap on inbound remittance to an NRE account. Resident savings deposits above INR 10 lakh in a financial year get reported to the Income Tax Department (SFT reporting) — not a problem, just something to be aware of.

If you're sending more than HKD 500,000 in one go, the smartest route is almost always your existing HK bank's remittance product, even if the FX margin is 20–40 bps worse than a fintech. The documentation audit trail is cleaner and the processing is more predictable.
